# Bylaw pattern: parking

## The shapes this bylaw takes

Parking bylaws depend on what the stalls legally are, and that is set by the strata plan, not the bylaw: a stall can be part of a strata lot, limited common property, common property, or a common asset, and each of those has a different answer. The shapes are: an assignment scheme, allocating common property stalls to lots; a visitor parking bylaw, with registration and time limits; a vehicle bylaw, restricting what may be parked (size, commercial vehicles, trailers, unlicensed or leaking vehicles) and forbidding repairs on site; a towing or immobilisation bylaw; and an EV charging bylaw dealing with installation, metering and the cost of electricity.

## What the Act and Regulation allow

The Act constrains assignment most tightly. Where a stall is common property that is not limited common property, or a common asset, the corporation may give an owner or tenant exclusive use or a special privilege, but only for up to one year at a time, renewable, with conditions, and cancellable on reasonable notice — not permanently [bc.spa.s76]. The one exception is prescribed: a stall where EV charging infrastructure was installed following an owner's request may be granted for 5 years [bc.spr.s5.101]. A permanent allocation has to be done by designating the stall limited common property, which is a different and more formal step [bc.spa.s73]. Charging for parking is a user fee, and a strata corporation must not impose user fees for the use of common property or common assets other than as the Regulation allows: the fee must be reasonable and set out in a bylaw, or in a rule that has been ratified [bc.spa.s110, bc.spr.s6.9]. A significant change in the use or appearance of common property needs a 3/4 vote, or a majority vote where it relates to EV charging infrastructure or managing the electricity it uses [bc.spa.s71]. Rules can reach common property stalls but not stalls inside a strata lot [bc.spa.s125]. Towing rests on the general bylaw power [bc.spa.s119] and on law outside the Act, so it is the shape most exposed to the limit that a bylaw is unenforceable to the extent it contravenes another enactment or law [bc.spa.s121].

## Where these bylaws go wrong

Treating a long-standing stall assignment as permanent when it is a year-to-year permission under [bc.spa.s76]; charging for a common property stall without a bylaw or a ratified rule setting a reasonable fee [bc.spr.s6.9]; reassigning stalls as a sanction, which is neither a fine nor a denial of a recreational facility and is not one of the Act's enforcement options; and towing from common property without the notice the bylaw and the general law require. Where a stall is limited common property or part of a strata lot, a bylaw that purports to reassign it is reaching property the corporation does not control.
