# Bylaw pattern: move-in and move-out

## The shapes this bylaw takes

Two shapes, and the difference between them decides whether a charge is lawful. A procedure bylaw: notice of a move, booking the elevator or loading bay, protective padding, permitted hours, and a requirement to make good any damage. And a charge bylaw: a move-in or move-out fee, sometimes framed as a damage deposit that is refundable, sometimes as a flat administration fee. Buildings often put the procedure in rules and the fee in a bylaw.

## What the Act and Regulation allow

A move fee is a user fee for the use of common property, and the rule is restrictive: a strata corporation must not impose user fees for the use of common property or common assets other than as set out in the Regulation [bc.spa.s110], and the Regulation permits one only if the amount is reasonable and it is set out in a bylaw, or in a rule that has been ratified at a general meeting [bc.spr.s6.9]. The fee may be a fixed amount or set on a reasonable basis such as the recovery of operating or maintenance costs, the duration of use or the number of users [bc.spr.s6.9]. So an unratified rule cannot support a move fee [bc.spa.s125], and a fee set to deter moving rather than to recover cost is exposed on reasonableness. A charge for damage is not a user fee and not a fine; a fine is capped by the bylaws and the Regulation [bc.spa.s132, bc.spr.s7.1]. Note the rental limit here too: a move-in procedure that in substance screens or approves tenants, or requires terms in a tenancy agreement, runs into the prohibition on restricting rentals [bc.spa.s141, bc.spa.s121].

## Where these bylaws go wrong

Charging a move fee under an unratified rule; charging a fee that is not tied to any cost the building can explain; holding a damage deposit as if it were a fine, or applying it without the complaint and answer process where it is in truth a penalty [bc.spa.s135]; and using the move-in form to collect tenant approval or screening information [bc.spa.s141]. A fee charged to a tenant rather than the owner also needs care: the Act's collection machinery runs against owners.
