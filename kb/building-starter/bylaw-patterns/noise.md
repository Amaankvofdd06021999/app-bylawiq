# Bylaw pattern: noise

## The shapes this bylaw takes

The standard use bylaw already forbids unreasonable noise and unreasonable interference with others' use and enjoyment [bc.spa.sched.bylaw3], so noise bylaws are additions to it rather than replacements. Four shapes: quiet hours, giving a window in which any audible noise is a contravention; a flooring bylaw, requiring carpet over a stated percentage of a floor or an underlay of a stated rating; an appliance or installation bylaw, dealing with hard flooring, washing machines and heat pumps; and a specific-activity bylaw covering renovations, musical instruments or parties. Many buildings put the detail in rules rather than bylaws.

## What the Act and Regulation allow

The general bylaw power supports all of these [bc.spa.s119], and the practical limits are about drafting rather than prohibition. Rules are a weaker instrument than bylaws and a different one: they can only govern the use, safety and condition of common property and common assets, so a rule cannot regulate noise made inside a strata lot; a bylaw prevails over a conflicting rule; and a new rule ceases to have effect at the first annual general meeting after it is made unless ratified [bc.spa.s125]. A bylaw is unenforceable to the extent it contravenes the *Human Rights Code* [bc.spa.s121], which is why a noise complaint about conduct connected to a disability, or about a child, is not an ordinary noise complaint. Flooring requirements interact with the alteration bylaws, because replacing a floor covering may also need approval [bc.spa.sched.bylaw5]. Amendments need a 3/4 vote and filing [bc.spa.s128].

## Where these bylaws go wrong

Putting a noise standard for inside a strata lot in a rule instead of a bylaw; relying on an unratified rule; treating quiet hours as the whole test, so that noise outside those hours is ignored even where it is unreasonable; and enforcing on a single complaint without the written particulars and opportunity to answer the Act requires [bc.spa.s135]. A noise bylaw that sets a decibel level a building cannot measure is unenforceable in practice, whatever its status in law.
